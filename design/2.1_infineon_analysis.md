# **2 · Reasoning & calculations**

The datasheet gives raw ingredients at its own test conditions. The engineering step is to convert these into application-specific loss figures at 200 V / 10 A / f_sw. Two loss families are computed: conduction and switching.

## **2.1 · Conduction loss**

A conducting MOSFET behaves as a resistor R_ds(on). Loss is I²R, using the HOT resistance (175 °C), never the 25 °C value — SiC R_ds(on) rises ≈1.4× with temperature.

_P_cond = R_ds(on)@175°C × I_rms²_

**Step 1 — why I_rms, not the 10 A peak.** What heats a resistor is the mean of the square of the current, so conduction loss is driven by the RMS current, not the peak. The 10 A is the peak drain current while the device is fully on — but the MOSFET conducts only for a fraction D of each switching period (its duty cycle) and carries zero the rest of the time.

**Step 2 — I_rms for a hard-switched (rectangular) current.** Modelling the drain current as a pulse of amplitude I_peak present for D·T and zero for (1−D)·T, the RMS definition gives:

_I_rms = √\[ (1/T) ∫ i² dt \] = √( I_peak² · D ) = I_peak · √D_

The period T cancels — I_rms depends on the fraction of time conducting, not the frequency.

**Step 3 — operating assumption: D = 0.5.** A duty cycle of 0.5 is chosen for the device-selection stage. It is the natural operating point of a symmetric half-bridge and, crucially, it is applied identically to all three candidates — so the conduction-loss comparison isolates the effect of R_ds(on) (the parameter under evaluation) rather than the operating scenario.

_I_rms = 10 · √0.5 = 7.07 A_

**Step 4 — conduction loss.**

_P_cond = 0.067 × (7.07)² = 0.067 × 50 = 3.35 W_

**Scope note (to state in the report):** _D = 0.5 is a deliberately simplified condition for ranking. A realistic sinusoidal-current inverter model (variable duty over the fundamental cycle, worst-case operating point) will be applied to the_ **_selected_** _device in the thermal-validation stage, using the measured E_on/E_off from the DPT rather than datasheet estimates._

## **2.2 · Switching loss — deriving E_on / E_off**

During a transition, V and I overlap → energy is dissipated. First-order (triangular) estimate:

_E_on ≈ ½ · V_DS · I_D · t_r E_off ≈ ½ · V_DS · I_D · t_f_

**Step 1 — base energies at datasheet conditions (400 V, 20.1 A):**

_E_on ≈ ½ (400)(20.1)(12.6 ns) = 50.7 µJ_

_E_off ≈ ½ (400)(20.1)(13 ns) = 52.3 µJ_

**Step 2 — rescale to the application point.** Switching energy scales linearly with both voltage and current (E ∝ V·I), because it is a V·I·t area. Transition times are held constant (same R_g family) — a first-order assumption.

_k = (V_your/V_ds) × (I_your/I_ds) = (200/400) × (10/20.1) = 0.5 × 0.498 = 0.249_

**Step 3 — energies at 200 V / 10 A:**

_E_on ≈ 50.7 × 0.249 = 12.6 µJ E_off ≈ 52.3 × 0.249 = 13.0 µJ_

_E_on + E_off ≈ 25.6 µJ per cycle_

**Step 4 — convert to power at the switching frequency:**

_P_sw = (E_on + E_off) × f_sw_

**Honest caveats (to state in the report):**

- t_r/t_f are voltage rise/fall times, not the exact V–I overlap window → the ½VIt figure is an estimate, adequate for comparing candidates.
- Transition times assumed constant across the rescale; in reality they vary with current and R_g.
- The DPT exists precisely to replace these estimates with measured E_on/E_off.

## **2.3 · Which frequency**

P_cond is frequency-independent; P_sw grows linearly with f_sw. Report both extremes and design against the 100 kHz worst case (highest switching loss → safest thermal margin).
